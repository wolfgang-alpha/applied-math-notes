The finite element method

Local basis functions, sparse matrices and the conjugate gradient method

Lecture 2: the finite element method is the Galerkin method of lecture 1 with local basis functions. The picture: a triangulation and the basis function h_i of one node, a pyramid of height 1 over the triangles around the node and zero everywhere else.

The whole lecture in one line. The Galerkin method of lecture 1 leaves open which basis functions to use; the finite element method uses local ones, which makes K sparse and allows iterative solvers whose work grows only like n.

Theory

Global or local basis functions

The choice of $h_1, \dots, h_N$ decides how well $u_h$ approximates $u$ and what the matrix $K$ looks like.

Global basis functions

nonzero on the whole domain, e.g. the cubic Hermite polynomials of lecture 1. Every $K_{ji} \neq 0$: $K$ is dense.

Local basis functions

nonzero only on a small part of the domain. $a(h_i, h_j) = 0$ if the supports do not overlap: $K$ is sparse.

The finite element method (FEM) is the Galerkin method with local basis functions.

Global basis functions are nonzero on the whole domain, like the cubic Hermite polynomials of exercise 2 of lecture 1: every entry K_ji is nonzero, K is dense. Local basis functions are nonzero only on a small part of the domain; a(h_i, h_j) = 0 if the supports do not overlap, so K is sparse. Highlighted in blue: one basis function and the row and column of K that belong to it.

Theory

The mesh and the coincidence table

The domain is approximated by a mesh, $\Omega \approx \Omega_h = \bigcup_e T_e$: a union of elements $T_e$ (intervals, triangles, tetrahedra) that meet at their vertices, the nodes. On each element $u_h$ is a polynomial of low degree.

In 1D the nodes $x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_N$ divide the interval into the elements $T_e = [x_{e-1}, x_e]$ of length $\ell_e = x_e - x_{e-1}$. Each element numbers its two nodes locally, 1 (left) and 2 (right).

Theory

Hat functions in 1D

The hat function of node $i$:

$$h_i(x) = \begin{cases} (x - x_{i-1}) / \ell_i & \text{for } x_{i-1} \le x \le x_i , \\ (x_{i+1} - x) / \ell_{i+1} & \text{for } x_i \le x \le x_{i+1} , \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
  • $h_i(x_j) = \delta_{ij}$, so $U_i = u_h(x_i)$: $u_h$ is the piecewise linear interpolation of the nodal values.
  • On $T_e$ only the hat functions of its two nodes are nonzero. In local numbering, $h_\alpha$ is the hat function of the local node $\alpha$: on $T_e$, $h_1 = (x_e - x) / \ell_e$ and $h_2 = (x - x_{e-1}) / \ell_e$.
Each basis function belongs to one node: it equals 1 there and 0 at all other nodes, and it is linear on each element. Hence the coefficient U_i is the value of u_h at node i. On an element only two hat functions are nonzero; in local numbering they are called h_1 and h_2 (alpha = 1, 2). The local h_alpha of T_e is the global hat function of node e - 2 + alpha, restricted to T_e.

Theory

Element matrices and assembly

For $a(u, v) = \int u'\, v' \; dx$ and constant $f$, on the element $T_e$:

$$k^e = \left( \int_{T_e} h_\alpha'\, h_\beta' \; dx \right)_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{1}{\ell_e} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$ $$f^e = \left( \int_{T_e} f\, h_\alpha \; dx \right)_\alpha = \frac{f\, \ell_e}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Assembly. $K$ is the sum of the element matrices. The coincidence table says where: $k^e_{\alpha\beta}$ is added to $K_{e-2+\alpha,\; e-2+\beta}$ and $f^e_\alpha$ to $F_{e-2+\alpha}$. Node $i$ shares an element only with $i \pm 1$, so $K$ is tridiagonal.

$h\, K$ for 5 elements of length $h$

The interior rows are the finite difference approximation $(-U_{i-1} + 2 U_i - U_{i+1}) / h^2$ of $-u''$, multiplied by $h$. The Dirichlet conditions replace the rows of the boundary nodes.

On T_e only the two local hat functions are nonzero, with derivatives -1/l_e and 1/l_e. This gives the 2 by 2 element stiffness matrix and the element load vector. The global matrix is the sum of the element matrices, each added at the rows and columns given by the coincidence table. The blocks overlap on the diagonal, so the interior diagonal entries are 2/h. The result is the finite difference matrix, multiplied by h.

FEniCS example 1

Hat functions and a projection

The Galerkin method with $a(u, v) = \int u\, v \; dx$ and $L(v) = \int \sin(\pi x)\, v \; dx$ gives $M\, U = F$, with the mass matrix $M_{ji} = \int h_i\, h_j \; dx$.


mesh = IntervalMesh(5, 0.0, 1.0)
V    = FunctionSpace(mesh, 'Lagrange', 1)

u, v = TrialFunction(V), TestFunction(V)
u_ex = Expression('sin(pi*x[0])', degree=4)
M    = assemble(u*v*dx)       # mass matrix
F    = assemble(u_ex*v*dx)    # load vector
uh   = Function(V)
solve(M, uh.vector(), F)

K = assemble(inner(grad(u), grad(v))*dx)
print(K.array())              # tridiagonal, 1/h = 5

Unlike the interpolation, $u_h$ does not take the nodal values of $u$: $U_i = 0.983$ at $x = 0.4$, but $\sin(0.4\pi) = 0.951$. $M$ is assembled from $m^e = \frac{\ell_e}{6} \left( \begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix} \right)$.

The dashed lines are the scaled basis functions U_i h_i; their sum is u_h. Theory

The linear triangle (CST)

On a triangle with the vertices $(x_\alpha, y_\alpha)$, $\alpha = 1, 2, 3$ (counterclockwise), $u_h$ is linear and fixed by the three nodal values:

$$u_h = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^{3} U_\alpha\, h_\alpha , \qquad h_\alpha(x, y) = \frac{a_\alpha + b_\alpha\, x + c_\alpha\, y}{2A}$$ $$a_1 = x_2\, y_3 - x_3\, y_2, \quad\ b_1 = y_2 - y_3, \quad\ c_1 = x_3 - x_2$$

and the other coefficients by cyclic permutation of the indices; $A$ is the area of the triangle.

$h_\alpha(x_\beta, y_\beta) = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$ and $h_1 + h_2 + h_3 = 1$ (area coordinates): as in 1D, $h_\alpha$ is the hat function of the local node $\alpha$, restricted to the triangle.

The linear triangle: u_h is linear on the triangle, a + b x + c y, and fixed by its three nodal values. The local basis function h_alpha is 1 at vertex alpha and 0 at the other two; its level lines are parallel to the opposite edge. The three functions sum to 1 and are also known as area (barycentric) coordinates.

Theory

Element matrices of the CST

Constant gradients

$$\nabla h_\alpha = \frac{1}{2A} \begin{pmatrix} b_\alpha \\ c_\alpha \end{pmatrix}$$

constant on the triangle, hence the name constant strain triangle (exercise 2)

Element stiffness matrix

$$k^e_{\alpha\beta} = \int_{T_e} \nabla h_\alpha \cdot \nabla h_\beta \; dx = \frac{b_\alpha\, b_\beta + c_\alpha\, c_\beta}{4A}$$

Element load vector, constant $f$

$$f^e_\alpha = \int_{T_e} f\, h_\alpha \; dx = \frac{f A}{3}$$

The global basis function $h_i$ of node $i$ is a pyramid of height 1 over the patch of triangles around node $i$.

Assembly works as in 1D, with the coincidence table of the mesh: three global node numbers per element, and $k^e$ is $3 \times 3$.

Since h_alpha is linear, its gradient is constant on the triangle. In elasticity the strain is built from the gradients of the displacement, so it is constant on the element: constant strain triangle. The integrand of k^e is constant, so the integral is the area times the integrand. For constant f, each of the three local basis functions integrates to A/3 (a pyramid of height 1 over the triangle has volume A/3).

Model problem

A uniformly heated square plate

The edges are kept at temperature 0:

$$-\nabla \cdot \nabla u = 1 \quad \text{in } \Omega = (-1, 1)^2, \qquad u = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial\Omega$$

The temperature at the centre follows from a Fourier series (exercise 4):

$$u(0, 0) = \frac12 - \frac{16}{\pi^3} \sum_{k = 1, 3, 5, \dots} \frac{(-1)^{(k-1)/2}}{k^3 \cosh(k \pi / 2)} = 0.294685413\dots$$

This exact value measures the error of the FEM solutions in examples 3 and 5.

The model problem for the 2D examples: a square plate, heated uniformly with source 1, with the edges kept at temperature 0 (Dirichlet). There is no closed-form solution, but the centre temperature follows from a Fourier series that converges very fast; exercise 4 derives it.

Theory

Sparsity

$K_{ij} = a(h_i, h_j)$ is nonzero only if the nodes $i$ and $j$ belong to a common triangle. A row of $K$ has about seven nonzero entries, independent of the size of the mesh. For $n$ unknowns:

stored entriesmemory for $n = 10^4$memory for $n = 10^6$
dense matrix$n^2$0.8 GB8 TB
sparse matrix (CSR)$\approx 7 n$0.8 MB84 MB

Direct solvers: fill-in

Gaussian elimination creates new nonzero entries in the triangular factors of $K$. Even with reordering, a sparse direct solver needs about $n^{1.5}$ operations in 2D and $n^2$ in 3D.

Iterative solvers

only need products $K\, p$: about $7 n$ operations each, and $K$ is never changed.

Two basis functions overlap only if their nodes share a triangle, so a row of K has about seven entries, independent of the mesh size. A dense matrix with n = 10^6 would need 8 TB; the sparse one in CSR format (value and column index of each entry) 84 MB. Gaussian elimination destroys the sparsity: fill-in. Iterative solvers only multiply with K.

Theory

Solving $K\, U = F$ means minimizing

$K$ is symmetric and positive definite. Solving $K\, U = F$ is then the same as minimizing

$$\Phi(U) = \tfrac12\, U^T K\, U - U^T F, \quad\ \nabla \Phi(U) = K\, U - F = -r$$

with the residual $r = F - K\, U$.

  • Steepest descent: $U_{k+1} = U_k + \alpha_k\, r_k$ with $\alpha_k = r_k^T r_k / r_k^T K\, r_k$. It zigzags for an ill-conditioned matrix.
  • Conjugate gradient (CG) method: the search directions $p_k$ are $K$-conjugate, $p_j^T K\, p_k = 0$ for $j \neq k$. After $k$ steps $U_k$ minimizes $\Phi$ over $U_0 + \operatorname{span}\{r_0, K r_0, \dots, K^{k-1} r_0\}$ (Krylov space); the solution is reached after at most $n$ steps.
For a symmetric positive definite K, K U = F is the condition for the minimum of the quadratic function Phi; the residual is the negative gradient. Steepest descent goes downhill along the residual with the optimal step length, but in a long narrow valley it zigzags. CG chooses K-conjugate directions; in 2 dimensions it reaches the minimum in 2 steps. The figure uses a 2 by 2 matrix with condition number 8, not the one of exercise 3.

Theory

The conjugate gradient method

$$\begin{aligned} & r_0 = F - K\, U_0, \quad p_0 = r_0 \\ & \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \dots \\ & \qquad \alpha_k = r_k^T r_k \,/\, p_k^T K\, p_k \\ & \qquad U_{k+1} = U_k + \alpha_k\, p_k, \qquad r_{k+1} = r_k - \alpha_k\, K\, p_k \\ & \qquad \text{stop if } \|r_{k+1}\| \le \mathrm{tol}\, \|F\| \\ & \qquad \beta_k = r_{k+1}^T r_{k+1} \,/\, r_k^T r_k, \qquad p_{k+1} = r_{k+1} + \beta_k\, p_k \end{aligned}$$
  • Each iteration needs one product $K\, p_k$: about $7 n$ operations.
  • $K$ is never changed, so there is no fill-in.
  • The new direction $p_{k+1}$ is the residual, made $K$-conjugate to $p_k$ by adding $\beta_k\, p_k$.
The algorithm: alpha_k is the optimal step length along p_k, the residual is updated with the product K p_k that is needed anyway, and beta_k makes the new direction K-conjugate to the previous one; it then is K-conjugate to all earlier directions as well. Only the vectors U, r, p and K p are stored.

Theory

Convergence and preconditioning

With the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min}$ of $K$:

$$\|U - U_k\|_K \le 2 \left( \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1} \right)^k \|U - U_0\|_K , \qquad \|e\|_K = \sqrt{e^T K\, e}$$

For element size $h$, $\kappa \sim h^{-2}$: the number of iterations grows like $1/h$, in 2D like $\sqrt n$, and the total work like $n^{1.5}$.

Preconditioning

Solve $M^{-1} K\, U = M^{-1} F$ with $M \approx K$ cheap to invert: the diagonal of $K$ (Jacobi), incomplete Cholesky.

Algebraic multigrid (AMG)

The work is proportional to $n$: the number of iterations stays constant. In FEniCS:


solver_parameters={'linear_solver': 'cg',
                   'preconditioner': 'amg'}
The error in the energy norm decreases at least by the factor (sqrt(kappa) - 1)/(sqrt(kappa) + 1) per iteration. For the stiffness matrix kappa grows like 1/h^2, so the number of iterations grows like 1/h. A preconditioner M approximates K and is cheap to invert; the iteration then sees the condition number of M^-1 K. Algebraic multigrid keeps it bounded independently of h.

FEniCS example 5

CG for the heated plate


for N in [8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512]:
    mesh = RectangleMesh(Point(-1.0, -1.0), Point(1.0, 1.0), N, N)
    V    = FunctionSpace(mesh, 'Lagrange', 1)
    u, v = TrialFunction(V), TestFunction(V)
    bc   = DirichletBC(V, Constant(0.0), 'on_boundary')
    K, F = assemble_system(inner(grad(u), grad(v))*dx,
                           Constant(1.0)*v*dx, bc)

    for preconditioner in ['none', 'amg']:
        uh = Function(V)
        solver = KrylovSolver(K, 'cg', preconditioner)
        solver.parameters['relative_tolerance'] = 1e-10
        iterations = solver.solve(uh.vector(), F)

The residual is reduced by a factor $10^{10}$. The error of the centre temperature drops from $3.6 \cdot 10^{-3}$ ($N = 8$) to $8.9 \cdot 10^{-7}$ ($N = 512$): like $h^2$.

Without preconditioner the iterations double with every halving of $h$; with AMG they stay at about 6.

Summary

The finite element method in four steps

  1. Mesh the domain: nodes, elements $T_e$ and the coincidence table (mesh.cells()).
  2. One local basis function per node with $h_i(x_j) = \delta_{ij}$, so $U_i = u_h(x_i)$: hat functions in 1D, pyramids over the patch on triangles.
  3. Compute the element matrices $k^e$, $f^e$ and add them into $K$ and $F$ with the coincidence table. $K$ is sparse, about 7 entries per row.
  4. Solve $K\, U = F$ with CG. Without preconditioner the iterations grow like $1/h$; with AMG they stay constant: {'linear_solver': 'cg', 'preconditioner': 'amg'}.
The four steps of the lecture. FEniCS does steps 1 to 3 in assemble or solve; step 4 is chosen with the solver parameters. The exercises compute the CST by hand, apply it to elasticity, run CG by hand and derive the exact centre temperature of the model problem.
